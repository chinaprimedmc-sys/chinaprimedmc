## Draft

Traveling in China with a food allergy or dietary restriction is possible, but the plan must be more specific than “no nuts,” “vegetarian” or “gluten free.” Identify the exact ingredients and cross-contact risks, carry a professionally translated card and your prescribed medication, choose suitable kitchens before the day, and keep a simple backup meal available. A guide can improve communication; no guide can guarantee that an ordinary shared kitchen is allergen-free.

For a severe allergy or medical diet, speak with your clinician before travel and follow your own emergency action plan. This article explains travel operations, not diagnosis or treatment.

## Separate medical risk from preference

Tell the planner which category applies:

- A diagnosed allergy with a risk of a serious reaction.
- Celiac disease or another medically required diet.
- An intolerance that causes symptoms but not anaphylaxis.
- A religious restriction.
- Vegetarian or vegan eating.
- A personal preference or food aversion.

The same ingredient may require completely different handling. A traveler who dislikes peanuts can skip a dish. A traveler at risk of anaphylaxis needs an emergency plan, medication and a kitchen decision. Combining both requests under “special meals” makes the serious requirement less visible.

## Build a precise bilingual food card

The card should state the ingredient in English and simplified Chinese, explain whether cross-contact is dangerous, list common forms of the ingredient, and say what to do if symptoms begin. Include a local emergency contact and the number 120 for ambulance services in mainland China.

Carry the card on paper and offline on the phone. Show it before ordering, not after the food arrives. A translation app can help with a conversation, but a saved, checked statement is more dependable when the battery is low or the restaurant is busy.

For a severe allergy, the card should not simply say “I cannot eat peanuts.” It should explain whether peanut oil, shared oil, sauces, garnish, stock and shared utensils are also unsafe. Have a qualified translator review the wording; do not rely on an unverified machine translation for a life-threatening condition.

## Understand where hidden ingredients appear

Chinese meals frequently use sauces, broths, marinades, spice mixtures and shared dishes. Wheat can appear in soy sauce, wrappers and noodles. Sesame may appear as oil, paste or garnish. Peanuts can appear whole, crushed, in sauces or in oil. Shellfish can be present in dried seasoning or stock. A vegetable dish may use animal stock or shared cooking oil.

The point is not that every dish is unsafe. It is that appearance and menu titles do not reveal the whole kitchen process. When the risk is serious, choose restaurants that can answer ingredient and cross-contact questions rather than trying to negotiate after a full table has been ordered.

## A safer restaurant decision sequence

1. Identify restaurants near the hotel and on long sightseeing days.
2. Contact the restaurant in advance with the exact requirement.
3. Ask whether the kitchen can prepare a simple meal separately.
4. Confirm again at the restaurant with the same written card.
5. Keep prescribed medication on the traveler, not in the vehicle or checked bag.
6. Carry a known safe snack or meal for transfers and unexpected closures.
7. Leave if the staff cannot confidently explain the preparation.

“We will tell the chef” is not the same as a cross-contact plan. For high-risk travelers, an apartment-style room with basic self-catering, a hotel willing to prepare a defined breakfast, or selected restaurants with simpler processes may be more useful than trying to sample every regional specialty.

## Hotel breakfast needs its own plan

Buffets create choice but also shared utensils, unclear labels and cross-contact. Ask whether the hotel can provide a separately prepared item and who should be contacted on arrival. If breakfast cannot be made suitable, plan an alternative rather than discovering the problem before an early train or Great Wall departure.

Packaged foods can be useful when the label is understood, but imported brands and formulations can differ. Do not assume that a familiar logo means identical ingredients. Keep enough safe food for travel days without trying to carry an entire trip's meals through customs.

## What changes on a private China journey

A private route can place meal decisions before the day begins. The guide can communicate the verified requirement, check whether the planned restaurant still agrees, help read labels and move to a fallback when the answer is unclear. The operations team can keep the same dietary briefing across cities instead of asking the traveler to restart the conversation at every handoff.

That support reduces uncertainty; it does not replace the traveler’s medication, clinician advice or emergency plan. AVIORA will not label an ordinary restaurant “allergy safe” without evidence the kitchen can meet the stated requirement.

## What to send a tour operator

Send one clear dietary profile for each traveler:

- Exact allergen or restriction.
- Severity and known reaction pattern.
- Cross-contact requirements.
- Medication the traveler will carry.
- Foods and cuisines that are reliably acceptable.
- Whether a self-catering option is needed.
- The traveler's own emergency instructions.

Ask the operator to state which meals are included, which restaurants are planned, what has been communicated, and what happens when a kitchen cannot meet the requirement. A promise that “the guide will handle it” is not a complete operating plan.

## Questions travelers ask

### Can restaurants in China accommodate severe allergies?

Some can, but capability varies by restaurant and kitchen. Confirm the exact ingredients and cross-contact requirement in advance. If the answer is uncertain, choose another kitchen or a controlled meal.

### Is vegetarian food easy to find in China?

Vegetable dishes are common, but they may contain animal stock, meat garnish or shared oil. Explain whether eggs, dairy, stock, oyster sauce and shared preparation are acceptable.

### Should I bring epinephrine or other allergy medicine?

Follow your clinician's advice. The CDC recommends carrying prescribed allergy medication, an emergency plan and translated allergy information, with extra supplies where local availability is uncertain.

### Can a private guide guarantee there will be no reaction?

No. A guide can communicate, check and help avoid unclear situations. Only the traveler and clinician can define the medical plan, and an ordinary restaurant may not be able to eliminate cross-contact.

### What is the emergency number for an ambulance in China?

In mainland China, call 120 for medical first aid. Keep the hotel address, local contact and insurance assistance number available offline.
